## The graveyard of unread MIS decks

Every finance leader has seen it: a 40-page Monday MIS pack with every conceivable cut of the P&L, circulated by email, opened by exactly two people. The accounts team spends half of Monday producing it. The C-suite glances at the first page and ignores the rest.

After a decade of shipping MIS workflows for SMEs, we've noticed the dashboards that *stick* share a small number of properties. This post is the short list.

## 1. One page. Always one page.

The moment MIS becomes two pages, the third page exists only for the person who built it. Pick the 5–8 numbers that matter most. Put them on one page. Put everything else on drill-down tabs that the reader can navigate if curious.

## 2. Same numbers, same layout, every week

Finance teams read Monday MIS the way a surgeon reads an X-ray — by habit and pattern. If you move the numbers around, you reset every reader's pattern-recognition. Pick a layout; keep it for six months; only change it when the business has materially changed.

## 3. Compare to one baseline, not three

Every stat should have a single comparator — last week, last month, or plan — whichever is most meaningful. Three comparators side-by-side is three conversations. Pick the most load-bearing one.

## 4. Variances, not levels

Humans are bad at absolute numbers and good at comparisons. A dashboard that shows "₹12.4L" revenue means little. "₹12.4L revenue, +8% vs prior Monday, +2% vs plan" means something. Lead with the variance; let the level appear as context.

## 5. Colour sparingly

Use colour only to flag exceptions. If every row is green / amber / red, the colour becomes decoration and the reader stops noticing. Reserve red for genuine red.

## 6. Narratives beat charts — for some readings

A two-line written narrative at the top ("This week: revenue up on back of 3 large sales, margin held, DSO drifted to 71 from 64 — this is the 3rd consecutive week rising, needs attention") beats any chart for the first-60-seconds reader. Charts are for the reader who wants to go deeper.

## 7. Make it easier to produce than to skip

The reason most MIS decks die is that producing them is manual, takes a day, and feels like stepping stones on month-end. Automate the production. ## A template

| Row | Cell |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **Revenue**: level · WoW variance · vs-plan variance |
| 2 | **Gross Margin %**: level · WoW delta |
| 3 | **Cash position**: current · vs end-of-last-month |
| 4 | **DSO / DPO**: levels · trend arrow |
| 5 | **Top-3 accounts overdue**: customer, amount, days overdue |
| 6 | **Exceptions this week**: 2–4 lines, anything non-routine |
| 7 | **Stock concern list**: 2 lines on slow-movers or stock-outs |
| 8 | **Cash runway**: months at current burn |

One page. Eight rows. Monday morning. Half the team reads it by 10:00. The other half starts asking questions in the stand-up.
